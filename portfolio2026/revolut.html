Value has to come before data

  1. The forces at play

    Value has to come before data

    Mapping the pains, gains, comforts and anxieties showed travellers are motivated by relevant results, but unsure what they get in return and not ready to hand over data before seeing value. Every ask needs a fair return.

  2. The opportunity

    Why would users want to sign up today?

    I ran a workshop on the value of signing in, bringing leadership and product together to map what travellers gain from an account: convenience, continuity, personalisation, access, and trust and safety. It surfaced the paradox at the heart of the problem.

  3. The measure

    Beyond accounts created

    Counting new accounts was the natural starting point. I took it a step further: logged-in session share shows whether travellers actually come back, which is where the revenue is.

The strategy in detail

Four pillars, following the traveller

Signing up has to feel like less effort than the reward. Two pillars shrink the effort, one grows the reward, and one makes every account worth having.

Sign-up and login prompts were inconsistent, and travellers rarely saw the benefits when they were relevant. I audited the messaging across every entry point and proposed a consistent, contextual presence.

From there, I made sure Google and Apple sign-in SDKs were available on the relevant devices, added clear feedback at every step so people always know what is happening, and created a space where travellers can access and manage their account once signed up.

Traveller profile
Before and after signing in

Asking travellers for their group size and children’s ages relies on their willingness to share. People give information when they get clear value in return, so every data request needs a fair exchange.

So instead of a cold form, we offer to save their preferences for future searches. It solves a problem customers have told us about (re-entering the same filters every time), it gives us richer data to personalise their experience and, in turn, it helps better monetise our platform.

What accounts unlock

Laying the foundations of a personalisation engine

An account is only worth having if Rome2Rio gets smarter the more you use it. I initiated the groundwork for a personalisation engine that brings together two things.

The signals we already have: searches, exits, dates and location, mapped to where they come from and whether we can use them today.

The signals we want to earn: rich traveller profiles and behaviour over time. Things like home city, travel party and preferred modes, but also seasonality and the types of trips people search for. Together they let us tailor ideas to who someone travels with and when, such as family-friendly trips timed to school holidays in their country. These signals only exist once people sign in.

Each signal then links to a concrete improvement at every stage of the trip, from dreaming to post-trip, so the experience feels more personal with every visit and gives travellers a reason to come back.

Case study · Afterpay (Block) · Still live

Afterpay Checkout

The checkout is the backbone of Afterpay’s Buy Now, Pay Later service. It lets customers split a purchase into manageable instalments, giving them flexibility and control over their money. Handling 15M+ sessions a month across Pay in 4 Standard, Express and Pay Monthly, it is Afterpay’s most lucrative revenue stream.

RoleLead product designer TeamPM, 6 engineers, risk and compliance
The problem

A framework built for Pay in 4, asked to do everything

The checkout started life for a single payment option. Pay Monthly arrived as a separate interface, and frequent regulatory updates were patched in with one-off components. For the Cash App convergence beta, we reskinned it as a stopgap, knowing it needed a real foundation.

Business problemNot built to scale
  • Each payment option had its own structure, fragmenting journeys and making them harder to maintain
  • No unified design to match Cash App’s goal of a cohesive architecture
  • Hard to adapt quickly to changing regulations across markets
Customer problemToo many choices, too little clarity
  • Inconsistencies caused confusion about amounts, terms and statuses
  • Friction lowered conversion and eroded trust
  • Checkout should feel fast and simple, on par with the market
The vision I setOne cohesive, dynamic checkout that adapts to every payment option
  • A scalable framework for all payment options
  • A simpler journey that reduces decision fatigue
  • Consistent design across products, aligned with Cash App’s Arcade system
The redesign

Clarity at the point of purchase

A legacy profile section wasted space, payment details lacked priority, and three legal disclaimers buried the confirm action. I set the direction to simplify it, remove clutter and rebuild it on standardised patterns.

BeforeAfter
+1.1%conversion in the US, ramped to 5% of traffic
86%of sessions on mobile, so mobile-first by default

Sources: experiment reporting and Amplitude.

  1. Merchant branding at the topReplacing the generic profile section with the merchant’s logo told customers where they were and who they were buying from.
  2. Receipt-style summary by the buttonThe total and the amount due today sit right above confirm, visible at the moment of decision.
  3. Three disclaimers become one lineWorked with risk and compliance to meet every legal requirement in a single, well-placed line.
  4. Plain-language errorsEvery error state rewritten with a specific cause and a clear way to recover.
The Arcade framework

From reskinned beta to a scalable system

I led the redesign of the summary screen, the core of checkout, on Cash App’s Arcade design system, so checkout feels like a natural extension of the merchant, with familiar sectioning across every plan.

Measured by task completion, comprehension and confidence, with our UXR partner.
