Rome2Rio · 2026

Multi-destination planning

People planning journeys with several stops had to leave the product and piece the legs together themselves. I brought multi-stop search into Explore so they can plan and compare a whole journey without leaving the view.

Feature design Search UX Usability testing

My role

Product Design Lead

Outcome

Consolidated multi-leg workflow into core product

Method

Usability testing, iterative design

The problem

Today's trip planner walks people through a single straight line, so planning a journey with several legs means leaving Explore and working out each leg by hand. You can't see all the legs at once or compare transport modes across the whole trip. Nothing is saved either so people who want to come back to a trip idea have to start again.

The insight

The trip planner worked fine but it was in the wrong place. If multi-stop lives in Explore people can see the whole journey, change it there and save it with the other things they collect for a trip like accommodation or attractions. The MVP only moves where multi-stop results appear and leaves the core logic as it is.

Usability testing: six key findings

1
Users expected to edit destinations inline Three of five participants tried to add or remove stops from the results panel and got frustrated when nothing happened. The design now has remove buttons on hover, drag handles to reorder and an Add a stop option below the timeline.
2
Trip total went unseen in the header Participants looked at each leg's price and added them up themselves instead of reading the total in the header. I moved the total price range to the footer just above the Save button so it sits next to the action it relates to.
3
Transport mode selection scope was unclear Participants didn't realise mode selection was per leg. I added a second panel showing routes for each leg as well as a Suggest options button that fills in the recommended option for every leg at once.
4
Map value depended on user experience Experienced travellers found the map a waste of space while less familiar people found it essential. The right panel now shrinks the map while people browse modes and times because location matters less there.
5
Save trip was discoverable and well received Saving worked well so I made it permanent as a labelled button in the sticky footer instead of an icon, which keeps it clear and visible however far people scroll.
6
Users naturally calculated journey totals Totals matter a lot. People added things up by hand, which tells us they need a total cost and time for the whole journey that is always visible.

What changed

Trip Overview. A short view of all the legs at once with start and end points, mode icons and the duration and price of each leg. People can see their whole journey and spot the most expensive or slowest leg straight away.

Inline destination editing. Each destination row responds on hover with a remove button, a drag handle and an Add a stop option below the timeline. People can rebuild their trip without leaving the results.

Mode selection panel. A second panel lists the available routes for each leg with a breakdown by mode. Suggest options fills in the recommended combination across every leg at once.

Persistent Save CTA. A labelled button in the sticky footer replaces the icon so saving is clear and always in view while people scroll through options.

"Bringing multi-stops into our core product meant moving a workflow, not building a new one. People show you where they expect to interact."

The vision beyond MVP

Multi-destination search is the starting point. Over time the trip planner menu can become a personal home for saved travel plans, whether that's a full multi-stop itinerary, a single route or a collection of destinations. Airbnb lets people save and review things as part of planning a trip in much the same way. Rome2Rio would move from a search tool to a place where people build their trips.
